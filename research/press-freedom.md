# Press freedom — RSF World Press Freedom Index (India)

## What it measures

The World Press Freedom Index (WPFI), published every year since 2002 by Reporters Without Borders (RSF, Reporters sans Frontières), compares the level of freedom enjoyed by journalists and media in 180 countries and territories. RSF defines press freedom as the ability of journalists, individually and collectively, to select, produce and disseminate news in the public interest independent of political, economic, legal and social interference and without threats to their physical and mental safety (rsf-methodology2025). India has been placed in the "very serious" band (score below 40) in the current scoring system (rsf-india).

## How it is built

- Published by RSF (Paris), annually; latest edition (2026) released around April 30, 2026 (journalism2026).
- Each country gets a 0–100 score (100 = best); countries are then ranked 1–180. Bands: 85–100 "good", 70–85 "satisfactory", 55–70 "problematic", 40–55 "difficult", 0–40 "very serious" (rsf-methodology2025).
- Score has two components: (a) a quantitative tally of abuses against journalists and media, and (b) a qualitative analysis based on responses of press-freedom specialists (journalists, researchers, academics, human-rights defenders) to an RSF questionnaire available in 25 languages, including Hindi (rsf-methodology2025).
- The questionnaire is organised into five contextual indicators, each scored 0–100 and weighted equally: political context, legal framework, economic context, sociocultural context, and safety. Within each indicator all questions and sub-questions have equal weight (e.g., political context has 35 questions/sub-questions, legal framework 26, economic context 26) (rsf-methodology2025).
- A seven-member expert panel (academics from LMU Munich, Reuters Institute, University of Miami, University of Cape Town, DW Akademie, plus RSF staff) oversees the methodology (rsf-methodology2025).
- The index is a snapshot of the previous calendar year, updated where the situation changes dramatically before publication (rsf-methodology2025).
- Before the 2022 methodology overhaul, the index used a different design — in the 2020 edition, six parameters and an 83-question questionnaire answered by a network of around 150 correspondents/researchers and 18 free-expression NGOs (niti2020).

## Credibility

Strengths:
- Mixes quantitative abuse data with qualitative expert assessment; covers 180 countries/territories with a transparent 0–100 scale and five published sub-indicators for every country (rsf-methodology2025; rsf-india).
- Methodology is periodically overhauled with an academic panel and the questionnaire is multi-lingual, including Hindi (rsf-methodology2025).

Criticisms:
- Small, anonymous respondent base: in the pre-2022 design roughly 150 correspondents/researchers and 18 NGOs answered ~83 questions per country — about one respondent per parameter per country — which NITI Aayog argued makes rankings "highly subjective at best"; question-wise and category-wise scores and the list of respondents are not published (niti2020).
- RSF hand-picks questionnaire respondents without published selection criteria, and data tabulation relies on a seven-member panel that critics describe as Western experts; critics argue the index is Western-centric (aljazeera2026).
- Ranks are not directly comparable across years: methodology and thresholds changed significantly in 2013 and 2022, and RSF itself had to recalibrate historical scores to make 25-year comparisons (rsf2026index).
- A rank can improve even as conditions worsen, because the rank is relative: India's 2024 rank rose while its score fell (thehindu2024).
- India-specific gaps: RSF's India page relies on general characterisations (e.g., "two to three journalists killed due to their work every year") without an India-year dataset in the public country page, and the government says the government's own data do not match RSF's narrative (thehindu2025; niti2020).

## Politics

- April 2020: after India slipped to 142, I&B Minister Prakash Javadekar said Indian media enjoys "absolute freedom" and criticised the report (toi2020; thehindu2020).
- NITI Aayog, the government's own think tank, published "Deciphering the World Press Freedom Index" questioning the survey's opaqueness, small respondent base and "perceived biases" (niti2020).
- The Centre has repeatedly dismissed international freedom rankings as "misinformed and propaganda driven" (thehindu2024).
- April 2, 2025: in a written Lok Sabha reply, Minister of State for Information & Broadcasting L. Murugan said "India has a vibrant press and media ecosystem, which does not need validation from foreign organisations," pointing to Article 19, the judiciary, the Press Council of India and self-regulation under the Cable Television Networks Act (thehindu2025).

## Real stories

- NewsClick raids and arrests: On October 3, 2023, the Delhi Police Special Cell raided the homes and offices of journalists, authors and academics linked to the news portal NewsClick, questioned 46 people, seized phones and laptops, and arrested founder-editor Prabir Purkayastha and HR head Amit Chakraborty under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, following an FIR registered on August 17, 2023 (thehindu-newsclick2023). On May 22, 2024, the Supreme Court called the arrest and remand illegal and ordered Purkayastha's release on bail; a Delhi court granted formal bail the same week (toi-bail2024; thehindu-release2024).
- NDTV takeover and media concentration: RSF's India profile says the 2022 acquisition of NTV-reputed channel NDTV by Gautam Adani's group — a business ally of PM Modi — "signalled the end of pluralism in the mainstream media", and flags highly concentrated ownership (Reliance's Mukesh Ambani owns more than 70 outlets) as a core reason for India's low rank (rsf-india).

## Gaps

- RSF has not published India's full score for the 2020–2022 editions in the sources consulted (only ranks were captured); the pre-2022 scores are on a different, reversed scale and RSF's own recalibrated series was not consulted.
- Exact publication date of the 2025 and 2026 indices from RSF itself was not captured directly; April 30, 2026 is per journalism.co.uk (journalism2026).
- The current questionnaire's total question count (sum across all five indicators, including sub-questions) was not captured — the methodology page was partially extracted; per-indicator counts (35/26/26/…) are cited instead.
