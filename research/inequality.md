# Inequality — World Inequality Lab, "The Rise of the Billionaire Raj"

## What it measures

This topic tracks how income and wealth are distributed in India, centred on the World
Inequality Lab (WIL) working paper "Income and Wealth Inequality in India, 1922–2023: The
Rise of the Billionaire Raj" (Bharti, Chancel, Piketty & Somanchi, March 2024). Its
headline finding: in 2022-23 the top 1% of Indian adults took 22.6% of national income and
held 40.1% of national wealth — both the highest levels ever recorded in their respective
series (since 1922 for income, since 1961 for wealth). The authors conclude the modern
"Billionaire Raj" is more unequal than the British colonial Raj (wid2024). Supporting data
come from official consumption surveys, which carry their own credibility problems: India
went eleven years without a published consumption survey (2011-12 to 2022-23) after the
2017-18 round was scrapped (mospi2024, thehindu2024).

## How it is built

- Published as World Inequality Lab Working Paper 2024/09 (March 2024); authors at Paris School of Economics, Harvard Kennedy School and NYU (wid2024, frontline2024).
- Method: Distributional National Accounts — combining national income accounts and wealth aggregates with income-tax tabulations, rich lists, and household surveys on income, consumption and wealth in one consistent framework (wid2024).
- The income series rests on annual income-tax tabulations published by the Indian tax authorities going back to 1922, when the Income Tax Act was enacted; the wealth series is anchored in Forbes/Hurun billionaire and rich-list data reconciled with national wealth aggregates (wid2024, indianexpress2024).
- Output metrics: shares of national income and national wealth held by the top 1%, top 10%, middle 40% and bottom 50%, plus average incomes/wealth per group (wid2024).
- The authors' own caveat: "the quality of economic data in India is notably poor and has seen a decline recently. It is therefore likely that our results represent a lower bound to actual inequality levels" (wid2024).
- It is a working paper, not peer-reviewed and not published in a journal — a point the government's Chief Economic Advisor made repeatedly in rebuttal (swarajya2024).

## Credibility

Strengths:
- The century-long income series is built from actual tax tabulations, not surveys — the only data source that captures the very rich, who are systematically missed in household surveys (wid2024, indianexpress2024).
- The method reconciles tax, rich-list and national-accounts data with surveys rather than trusting any one source; trends of top income and wealth shares track each other across the whole period, an internal consistency check (wid2024).
- The authors explicitly publish their uncertainty: estimates are "a lower bound to actual inequality levels" given declining Indian data quality (wid2024).

Criticisms:
- Chief Economic Advisor V. Anantha Nageswaran (with Deeksha Supyaal Bisht, Mint, 27 March 2024) argued the paper relies on outdated PLFS data, relegates the newer HCES 2022-23 to a footnote, double-counts market capitalisation as personal "wealth" from Forbes/Hurun lists, and mistakes improved tax compliance for rising inequality; he also noted rising tax collections reduce the consumption Gini via health/education spending, and cited NITI Aayog's finding that 248 million Indians escaped multidimensional poverty in a decade (swarajya2024).
- Former PMEAC member Surjit Bhalla (with Karan Bhasin, Indian Express, 27 March 2024) attacked the paper's footnote-admitted uncertainty and cited Auten-Splinter's finding that US top-1% post-tax shares barely moved, questioning the Piketty-style method globally (indianexpress2024c).
- Counter-response: the WIL authors say the critiques misread footnote 36 (which concerns only whether the tax code is regressive by wealth, not the inequality estimates) and call the rebuttals "textbook cases of inequality denial"; they note the top-1% share for 1999 is 14.7% in their series, not the inflated figure the critics attributed (indianexpress2024b).
- India's data gaps are real regardless of side: no official consumption survey was published for 2011-12 → 2022-23 (the 2017-18 round was junked in November 2019 citing "data quality" after a leaked draft showed the first fall in rural consumption in four decades); the decennial Census has not been conducted since 2011; and the 2022-23 survey changed recall methodology, complicating trend claims (indiaforum, sciencedirect2024, mospi2024b).

## Politics

- November 2019: the government decided not to release the NSS 2017-18 consumption survey; two National Statistical Commission members, including acting chair P.C. Mohanan, had already resigned in January 2019 amid related data-withholding disputes (indiaforum, sciencedirect2024).
- 16 January 2023: Oxfam's "Survival of the Richest: The India Story" (Davos release) reported India's top 1% held more than 40.5% of wealth in 2021 while the bottom 50% held ~3%, and urged wealth taxes; Oxfam India CEO Amitabh Behar said India was "on a fast track to becoming a country only for the rich" (oxfam2023, bbc2023).
- 1 January 2022: the Home Ministry denied renewal of Oxfam India's FCRA registration, cutting off foreign funding; in December 2022 the rejection was confirmed, and in February 2023 the ministry recommended a CBI probe into alleged FCRA violations (oxfam2022, scroll2023, thehindu2023).
- 27 March 2024: the CEA's Mint article "Ten reasons why Piketty's paper gets it wrong on inequality in India" (with Bisht) rebutted the Billionaire Raj paper, pointing instead to a doubling middle class (PRICE data) and multidimensional-poverty declines (swarajya2024).
- 27 March 2024 (same day): Bhalla-Bhasin's Indian Express column made parallel attacks; WIL authors responded in April 2024 defending the 22.6%/40.1% findings and accusing government-adjacent economists of denial (indianexpress2024c, indianexpress2024b).
- No PIB release disputes the Billionaire Raj paper directly; the official rebuttal channel was the CEA's op-ed and replies in the financial press (swarajya2024, indianexpress2024b).

## Real stories

- **The Ambani wedding, Mumbai, 12 July 2024.** Anant Ambani, son of Asia's richest man Mukesh Ambani, married after months of celebrations that Reliance itself promoted worldwide; Reuters noted the spectacle "overshadow[ed] some critical social media posts that have questioned the opulence at a time of growing inequality in India" (reuters2024). At the March 2024 pre-wedding gala the BBC reported Mukesh Ambani's net worth at $115bn per Forbes (bbc2024a); Quartzy reported expected spending of at least $132m on the wedding (qz2024). For scale: CNN noted an average Indian wedding costs about $15,000 — roughly three times the average annual household income (cnn2024) — while the WIL paper computes that India's ~10,000 wealthiest adults own an average ₹22.6bn each, 16,763 times the average Indian's wealth (wid2024).
- **Oxfam's Adani arithmetic, January 2023.** Oxfam's India supplement calculated that a one-off 20% tax on Gautam Adani's unrealised gains (2017-21) could have raised ₹1.79 lakh crore — "enough to employ more than five million Indian primary school teachers for a year" — and that 64% of the ₹14.83 lakh crore collected in GST in 2021-22 came from the bottom 50% of the population (economictimes2023, frontline2023).

## Gaps

- The paper's comparative top-1% income-share values for the US, Brazil and South Africa were not printed in the extracted sections (only the qualitative "higher than South Africa, Brazil and the US" claim and China's 15.7% are verified); per-country wealth shares beyond Brazil/South Africa top-10% (85.6%/79.7%) likewise unverified.
- The original CEA op-ed (Mint/Livemint, 27 March 2024) URL was not retrieved; its content is sourced via Swarajya's detailed summary plus the WIL authors' response in Indian Express.
- No official income-based Gini exists — the official Gini is consumption-based (HCES), and HCES 2022-23's MMRP methodology breaks strict comparability with 2011-12; the government's "declining inequality" claim therefore rests on a changed instrument.
- The leaked 2017-18 consumption estimates were never officially published; figures on what that survey showed come from secondary accounts (The India Forum; academic literature), not an NSO release.
- The IMF per-capita GDP figures cited are October 2020 WEO projections, not final outturns; later-vintage WEO numbers were not pulled for this file.
